Sudden High Water Bill in Mahopac, NY? Look for a Leak

A bill that jumps with no change in how you live is often a leak. Here is a simple way to check your meter, the places leaks hide and what to do when you find one.

By the Mahopac Water Damage Pros team. Published and updated November 12, 2026.

Why a bill spike is a clue

Water use is fairly steady month to month for most households. A bill that doubles with no guests, no pool fill and no garden watering is a signal. Hidden leaks run constantly and quietly, and a toilet flapper that leaks into the bowl can use hundreds of gallons a day.

A bill is also a delayed signal, because it arrives weeks after the leak starts. By then the damage may already be present in a wall, a ceiling or a floor.

A meter test you can do in ten minutes

1

Shut everything off

Turn off taps, dishwasher, washer and ice maker. Make sure no one flushes.

2

Read the meter

Note the numbers or the small leak indicator dial.

3

Wait and read again

Do not use water for ten to fifteen minutes, then read again.

4

Compare

If the number or dial moved, water is escaping somewhere.

If the meter is outside and the house has a main shutoff, close the main and repeat the test. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the house.

Mahopac notes

Water districts and wells in the Mahopac area

The Town of Carmel has water districts that serve parts of the town, and the county hazard plan refers to the Carmel water districts. Not every home is on a district, and many homes in the area use private wells. If you are on a well, you will not get a bill that reveals a leak, so the pump running more often or pressure dropping may be the clue.

Ask the Town of Carmel or your water provider which district serves your address and what its billing cycle is. If you live in Baldwin Place, the Town of Somers has its own districts, and Jefferson Valley is in the Town of Yorktown, so ask that town. See our pages for Baldwin Place and Jefferson Valley for which authority covers each.

If you find a leak

  • Close the nearest shutoff, or the main if you cannot find it.
  • Photograph the leak and any wet surfaces before touching them.
  • Call a plumber for the repair.
  • Check for wet drywall, flooring or cabinetry near the source.
  • Ask the water provider about leak adjustments on the bill, which some offer.

Outdoor and irrigation leaks

Not every leak is inside. A broken irrigation head, a stuck hose bib or a cracked line between the meter and the house can run for weeks without showing indoors. Soft ground, a patch of grass that stays green in a dry spell or a sound of running water near the meter are all clues.

If the main shutoff test shows that water is moving while the house is closed off, the leak is outside the house. A plumber with leak detection equipment can find it, and you will want it repaired before the ground freezes.

Toilets are the most common quiet leak

A toilet that leaks from the tank into the bowl makes little noise and can run for weeks. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper or fill valve is leaking.

Replacing a flapper is a small repair that most homeowners can do. If the leak continues after that, ask a plumber to check the fill valve and the flush valve seat.

Damage that comes with a long, slow leak

A leak that has run for weeks wets more than the area around the pipe. Drywall wicks upward, insulation holds water and subfloor swells. A moisture survey shows how far it went. Our wall and drywall water damage repair page explains the repair side of a wall leak, and structural drying explains drying.

Insurers often treat long-term seepage differently from a sudden break, so keep records of the bill history and the date you found the leak.
