What emergency water extraction removes and what it leaves behind

Extraction takes out the bulk water that you can see. Submersible pumps handle depth, and wet vacuums with extraction wands pull water from carpet, pad and hard floors. One inch of water across a 1,000 square foot floor is roughly 620 gallons, which is why a small pump and a shop vac rarely keep up.

What extraction cannot remove is the moisture already absorbed into drywall, framing and subfloor. That water has to be evaporated and pulled out of the air. A job that stops at extraction looks finished and then smells wrong within days, so we treat extraction as the first half of a two-part job.

Speed matters because of how water spreads. It wicks upward into drywall, runs under baseboards and drops through floor openings into the level below. Getting the bulk out early shrinks the area that has to be dried and, usually, the repair list.

How an extraction visit unfolds

1

Check hazards and source

We confirm the water supply is off and the electrical situation is safe, then identify where the water entered.

2

Remove standing water

Pumps clear depth first. Wet vacuums and wands then work through carpet, pad and low spots the pump cannot reach.

3

Map what is still wet

Moisture meters and a thermal camera show which walls, floors and ceilings absorbed water and need drying equipment.

Once the bulk water is gone, the same visit usually moves into setting air movers and dehumidifiers. Our structural drying and dehumidification page explains how long that phase takes and how readings guide it.

What changes the price of water extraction

Extraction is billed on the work involved, and the scope depends on a handful of facts that we can only confirm in the house.

Depth and volume

An inch of water in one room and a foot in a finished basement call for different pumps, hoses and crew time.

Water category

Clean supply water is straightforward. Water that has touched sewage or sat for days needs protective gear and disposal steps.

Surface mix

Carpet and pad, hardwood, tile and bare concrete each need their own technique and drying plan.

Access

Stairs, tight crawl spaces and furniture that has to be moved add handling time.

Time of the call

Overnight and holiday emergency work takes crews away from other jobs and is scoped accordingly.

What follows

Extraction usually continues into drying and sometimes demolition. Each phase is scoped so you can see where the cost sits.

We explain the scope and what drives it before work expands, and you decide what proceeds.

Mahopac notes

Water extraction around Lake Mahopac

Mahopac sits around a 587 acre lake and several smaller ones, so groundwater and storm runoff are ordinary facts of life for lower levels. The Putnam County hazard mitigation plan records that Hurricane Sandy blew out culverts in the Town of Carmel and flooded basements. It also names Memory Lane, where an undersized cross-culvert is part of the problem.

The July 2023 storms are a more recent example. Putnam County declared a state of emergency on July 10, 2023, after roads, bridges and culverts were damaged across the county. When roads wash out, basements are usually taking water too, and extraction calls come in clusters. If you can, call early and describe how deep the water is.

While you wait for the crew

  • Turn off power to the affected rooms at the breaker panel only if the panel itself is dry and you can reach it without stepping in water.
  • Move rugs, electronics, paper and small furniture to a dry level.
  • Photograph the water line on the walls and the source before anything is moved.
  • Leave carpet and pad in place. Lifting and pulling them is easier with the right tools and a plan for disposal.
  • Keep pets and children out of rooms with standing water.

Safety comes before speed

Water and electricity are the main danger. If water has reached outlets, baseboard heaters, the furnace or the panel, stay out and call. If a ceiling is sagging with trapped water, do not stand under it. A small hole lets the water drain in a controlled way, but we would rather you call first.

Reference standard

We use the ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage standard as a reference for how wet structures are evaluated and dried. That is a statement about the standard we follow, not a claim of any certification. Extraction is the first step, and the standard treats it as the start of drying rather than the end.
